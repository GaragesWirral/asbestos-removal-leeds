Asbestos in Older Leeds Homes: Where It Hides and What to Do

A plain guide for homeowners and landlords, based on guidance from the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) and GOV.UK.

Leeds is full of homes built long before 2000: Victorian terraces, interwar semis, postwar estates and 1970s bungalows. Many of them have been extended, rewired and redecorated over the years, and some of the materials used along the way may contain asbestos. This guide explains where it tends to turn up, why it matters, and what to do if you think you have found some. It is general information, not a substitute for a proper survey.

Could my house contain asbestos?

HSE says asbestos can be present in any building built or refurbished before 2000, and that using asbestos in the construction or refurbishment of buildings has been illegal since 1999 (HSE: why asbestos is dangerous and where it can be found). So the question to ask is not how old the street looks, but when the house was built and when it was last done up. A Victorian terrace that was refurbished in the 1980s can contain asbestos products from that refurbishment.

You cannot tell by looking. HSE is clear that you cannot see, smell or feel asbestos fibres in the air or on your clothes, and that the way to know whether a material contains asbestos is to have it surveyed or have samples analysed (HSE: identifying if asbestos is present).

Why it matters

Asbestos that is left alone and in good condition is low risk, according to HSE. The danger comes when it is disturbed or damaged and fibres are released and breathed in (HSE: guide to asbestos safety for workers). The diseases linked to asbestos, including mesothelioma, asbestosis and asbestos related lung cancer, often take a long time to develop and cannot be cured (HSE). HSE puts the toll at around 5,000 deaths a year in Great Britain.

That is why the most important moment is usually not when you first notice an old ceiling, but when you or a tradesperson are about to drill, sand, scrape or knock something out.

Where asbestos hides in a house

HSE publishes a diagram of a typical home showing where asbestos may be found (HSE: where asbestos can occur in buildings). Inside, the places it lists include:

  • Textured ceilings and walls, such as Artex.
  • Vinyl floor tiles backed with asbestos paper and bitumen adhesive.
  • Asbestos insulating board (AIB) around boilers, behind fires and fuse boxes, in airing cupboards, partition walls, ceiling tiles, bath panels and window panels. HSE notes that AIB closely resembles ordinary plasterboard (HSE asbestos FAQs).
  • Pipe lagging and sprayed insulation on boilers.
  • Toilet cisterns and cold water tanks, particularly older cement tanks in lofts.
  • Loose fill insulation in cavities.

Outside, it lists soffits and fascias, roofing felt, cement roof tiles and slates, cement panels, gutters and downpipes, and profiled cement sheeting. In Leeds that last one is most often seen on the roofs of detached garages, sheds and outbuildings.

HSE also has a gallery of real photographs of these materials, which is worth a look before you start a project (HSE: locations of asbestos and actions to take).

I think I have found asbestos. What now?

HSE's advice to members of the public (HSE asbestos FAQs: members of the public) comes down to a few points:

  • Do not try to repair or remove it yourself unless you have had training for non licensed asbestos work.
  • If it is in good condition and unlikely to be damaged, it is often best left where it is. Check its condition from time to time.
  • Slightly damaged material can sometimes be repaired by sealing or enclosing it, but only by someone with the right training.
  • Badly damaged material that is likely to get worse should be removed if it cannot be protected.
  • Tell any builder or tradesperson about asbestos materials before they start work, so they do not disturb them by accident.

You can also ask for advice from an environmental health officer at your local council. GOV.UK has a postcode tool that takes you to the right council page for asbestos information and disposal (GOV.UK: asbestos in your home).

Before you renovate: test first

Most problems happen during home improvements. Before you take down a ceiling, lift old floor tiles, remove a chimney breast or knock through a wall in an older house, find out what you are dealing with. HSE says that where there is no information you can have the area surveyed, have representative samples analysed, or simply assume the material contains asbestos and take the right precautions (HSE).

HSE describes two kinds of survey (HSE: arrange an asbestos survey). A management survey records the location and condition of asbestos during normal use of a building. A refurbishment or demolition survey is needed before major works; it looks for asbestos hidden in the fabric of the building, involves destructive inspection, and the area must be vacated while it is done. If you are planning a big project, it is the second kind you want.

Who can do the work?

The rules depend on the material and the job (HSE: deciding if a licensed contractor must do the work). Some lower risk tasks, such as drilling a textured coating to put up fixtures or fittings, or sealing asbestos that is in good condition, are classed as non licensed work, although they still have to be done by trained people using the right methods. Other jobs must be notified to the authorities even though no licence is needed, for example where cement sheeting will be substantially broken up.

Higher risk work must only be done by a contractor holding a licence from HSE. That includes any work on sprayed asbestos, most repair or any removal of asbestos insulation such as pipe lagging, and most work on asbestos insulating board. Whoever you hire, ask them to explain which category your job falls into and why.

Getting rid of it safely

Asbestos waste is hazardous waste. HSE says it must be legally disposed of as such and must not be mixed with normal household waste (HSE asbestos FAQs). Your council may offer a collection or a site that accepts it, so check through the GOV.UK council finder before you bag anything up.

One more point that catches people out when clearing an old house or garage: HSE says it is illegal to supply any article containing asbestos, whether for money or free of charge. Old cement sheets, tanks or boards should not be sold, given away or reused.

A quick checklist

  • Was the house built or refurbished before 2000? If yes, assume asbestos is possible.
  • Is the material in good condition and away from harm? Leaving it alone is often the safest choice.
  • Planning work that will disturb it? Get it tested or surveyed first.
  • Hiring someone? Tell them what you know, and check whether the job needs a licensed contractor.
  • Disposing of it? Treat it as hazardous waste and go through your council.
