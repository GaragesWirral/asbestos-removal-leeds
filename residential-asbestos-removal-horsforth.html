Residential Asbestos Removal in Horsforth, Serving England's Former Largest Village

Horsforth's status as England's largest village by population in the late Victorian era meant it developed a wide range of housing types, Victorian terraces near the town centre, large inter-war detached houses on the outer residential streets, and post-war private development on the LS18 periphery. Whether you are renovating an older Town Street terrace, extending a detached inter-war house off Low Lane, or dealing with a garage roof before a sale, Yorkshire Asbestos Solutions provides licensed residential removal across Horsforth and the LS18 area.

Horsforth appears in the Domesday Book as "Horseford", meaning "horses' ford," referring to a River Aire crossing used to transport woollen goods. In the late Victorian era it was famously the village with the largest population in England. Low Hall (16th century Tudor architecture) is one of the oldest surviving buildings in Horsforth and was home to the Stanhope family from 1565. In December 1642, Royalist troops from Leeds invaded Low Hall and stabled 200 horses in the barn during the English Civil War. Leeds Trinity University is based here, and the Brownlee twins, Olympic triathlon champions, were born and raised in Horsforth.

Local knowledge: In the late Victorian era, Horsforth was recognised as the village with the largest population in England, a remarkable distinction for what was still classed as a rural settlement.

Three High Streets and a Century of Housing: Horsforth's Asbestos Landscape

Horsforth has Victorian stone terraces in the older town centre areas, Edwardian and inter-war semis on established residential streets, and post-war private estates on the town's outskirts. Its popularity as a commuter suburb means properties have been widely extended and renovated.

Horsforth's mix of Victorian, Edwardian, and post-war housing creates varied asbestos risk across the town. Older terraces and semis frequently contain artex, soffits, and floor tiles from 1960s–80s renovations. Garage conversions and loft extensions, common in Horsforth's suburban semis, frequently encounter asbestos cement materials. A survey before any structural work is strongly recommended.

What Asbestos Is Found in Horsforth Houses?

Horsforth's housing ranges from Victorian and Edwardian terraces near the town centre to large inter-war and post-war detached and semi-detached properties on the outer residential streets. The range of housing eras is one of the widest of any Leeds suburb, meaning almost any property type, and almost any asbestos material type, can be encountered here.

  • Artex and textured coatings, on ceilings in Victorian terraces near Town Street and in post-war detached properties; applied at different times but consistently common across all housing eras in Horsforth
  • Asbestos insulation board (AIB), in airing cupboards, ceiling tiles, and fireplace surrounds of 1950s–70s semis and bungalows; one of the higher-risk residential ACMs
  • Asbestos cement garage roofs and soffits, standard on detached garages and fascia boards on pre-1985 houses; very common on the larger properties built for Horsforth's professional commuter population
  • Pipe lagging, on original plumbing in unmodernised Victorian and Edwardian terraces near Low Lane and Stanhope Drive
  • Vinyl floor tiles, with asbestos adhesive, found in service areas and under newer flooring in properties of all eras

Low Hall, a remarkable 16th-century Tudor building, one of the oldest surviving structures in the Leeds district, once home to the Stanhope family whose fortune shaped much of Horsforth's civic and industrial development, survives in a residential street as a reminder of quite how ancient this suburb's foundations are. Hall Park, created on the grounds of the demolished Horsforth Hall, borders Victorian and Edwardian terraces that give way to larger inter-war semis on the outer streets. St Margaret's Church (nave 1883, tower 1901) and Horsforth Museum, a volunteer-run collection documenting the town's history from its Domesday Book mention to the present, anchor a community whose housing spans more construction eras than almost any other Leeds suburb. That variety of building eras means the asbestos risk profile changes street by street, and knowing those eras is part of how we prepare for every Horsforth job.
