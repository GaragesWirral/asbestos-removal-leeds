Why Get an Asbestos Survey Before Buying a House?

Any home built before 2000 may contain asbestos. Most of the time that is not a reason to walk away. Asbestos in good condition is often perfectly safe to leave in place. What matters is knowing about it before you buy, especially if you are planning to renovate.

A standard RICS Home Survey or a mortgage valuation may mention materials that could contain asbestos, but they do not usually take samples or tell you for certain. That leaves you guessing about things like a textured ceiling, a garage roof or the boards in an airing cupboard.

An asbestos survey before buying a house fills that gap. You find out exactly what is present, where it is and what condition it is in, so you can plan your renovation, budget for any removal and negotiate with the seller if needed.

Worth a survey if

  • The house was built or extended before 2000
  • Your homebuyer survey flags possible asbestos
  • You plan a new kitchen, bathroom, loft or extension
  • There is an old garage, shed or outbuilding
  • You can see textured ceilings or old floor tiles
  • You want certainty before exchanging contracts
Home Surveys

Does a Building Survey Detect Asbestos?

Not reliably. A RICS Level 2 survey, often called a HomeBuyer report, and a Level 3 building survey are visual inspections of the property's condition. The surveyor may note possible asbestos, for example an Artex ceiling or asbestos cement roof sheets on a garage, and recommend further checks. But a house survey is not an asbestos survey. The surveyor does not usually take samples, and nothing is tested in a laboratory.

An asbestos surveyor does something different. They look specifically for materials that may contain asbestos, take samples safely, and have them analysed so you know for certain whether asbestos is present and what type it is. The HSE recommends using an asbestos surveyor from an organisation accredited by UKAS, and using a UKAS accredited laboratory for the analysis.

If your homebuyer survey says asbestos is noted or suspected, that is the moment to get an asbestos survey before you buy, while you still have time to ask questions and, if needed, renegotiate the price.

Asbestos is a naturally occurring mineral that was widely used as a building material because it is strong, cheap and resists heat. Blue and brown asbestos were banned in the UK in 1985 and white asbestos was banned in 1999, so any house or flat built before 2000 could contain it. A 1950s house, a 1970s semi or a 1990s new build are all worth checking if you plan any work. The risk comes from asbestos fibres released when materials are damaged or disturbed.

What We Check

What a Pre Purchase Asbestos Survey Covers

The survey is based on a management survey, adapted for a house that is still occupied by the seller. With the seller's permission, the asbestos surveyor inspects every accessible room, the loft, cupboards, garage and outbuildings, and takes small samples of suspect materials. Each sample point is sealed afterwards, and samples are analysed by a UKAS accredited laboratory.

  • Textured coatings on ceilings and walls
  • Floor tiles and adhesive in kitchens, bathrooms and hallways
  • Boards around boilers, airing cupboards and meter boxes
  • Cement garage roofs, sheds, soffits, gutters and flues
  • Old pipe lagging in cellars and lofts
  • Bath panels, window boards and fireplace surrounds

Because the house is someone else's home, the survey is not intrusive, and the surveyor will not disturb materials more than a small sample needs. If you are planning major work after you move in, we recommend a refurbishment survey of the affected rooms before your builders start.

Your Report

What Happens If Asbestos Is Found?

Your report lists every asbestos containing material found, with photographs, lab results and a plain English recommendation for each one. Typical outcomes are:

  • Nothing found: peace of mind for the areas inspected
  • Found, good condition: leave it, note it and avoid disturbing it
  • Found, will be disturbed by your plans: budget for removal before work
  • Found, damaged: plan for repair or removal soon after you move in

Using it in your purchase

Many buyers use the report to plan their first year in the house. Some use it to discuss the price with the seller, particularly if removal will be needed before a planned renovation. If you want, we can give you a written quote for any removal so you have a real figure rather than a guess.

Timing matters. Book the survey after your offer is accepted but before you exchange contracts, and allow time for the seller to agree access and for the lab results to come back.

Buying With Asbestos

Buying a House With Asbestos

Finding asbestos in a house you want to buy can feel alarming, but it is very common. Asbestos was used in UK homes for decades, with the heaviest use between the 1950s and the 1980s, until it was banned in 1999. Many perfectly good houses on the market today contain some.

Is it a deal breaker? Usually not. Asbestos in good condition, such as an intact Artex ceiling or a sound cement garage roof, can often be left alone. It becomes a cost when it is damaged or when your renovation plans would disturb it. A survey turns an unknown risk into a known cost.

Mortgages and value. Most mortgage lenders will lend on a property that contains asbestos, although a valuer may comment on it and some lenders may ask questions about damaged material. Knowing exactly what is there, and having a removal quote if needed, puts you in a stronger position with your lender and in any price negotiation with the seller.

Sellers and disclosure. Sellers should answer pre contract enquiries honestly and share any asbestos information they hold, such as an old survey or removal paperwork. Ask your conveyancing solicitor to request it. If there is none, a survey before you exchange is the way to find out.

Survey or test kit? A home asbestos test kit only checks the sample you send. A professional asbestos survey looks at the whole property, samples safely and gives you a report you can rely on when buying a house.

  • Artex and popcorn ceilings: often low risk if left undisturbed
  • Garage roofs: common, and usually straightforward to remove
  • Floor tiles: fine under new flooring, but avoid sanding or lifting
  • Boards around boilers: higher risk, check before any heating work
